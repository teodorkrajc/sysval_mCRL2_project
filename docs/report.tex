
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Designers and engineers have a long and complicated relationship. The designer dreams; the engineer has to find out how to make that dream come to life, a challenge ultimately grounded in reality. Touchpoint Towers is a fine (theoretical) example of this. Inspired by Michelangelo's \textit{The Creation of Adam} (Figure~\ref{fig:creation_of_adam}), a crazy architect designed its two buildings to lean towards each other and meet at a single floor where, unlike in the painting, they actually touch. This means two elevator rails cross at an intersection, presenting a dangerous possibility for crashes that must be dealt with. In this project, we will do the honors. 

Elevators are safety-critical, and a controller coordinating two of them is difficult to get right by testing alone: its components act concurrently, and failures tend to hide in rare interleavings that tests seldom reach. Formal methods address this by modelling the controller before it is built and checking its requirements against every possible behaviour of the model. For the course System Validation (2IMF30), we do this with the mCRL2 toolset~\cite{bunte2019mcrl2,groote2014modeling}, which describes a system as communicating processes, expresses requirements in the modal $\mu$-calculus, and is able to check automatically whether the model satisfies them. Our goal is a controller that keeps the intersection safe and serves every passenger, including those that travel between buildings. This presents a substantial modelling challenge: the two elevators compete for the touchpoint, which requires mutual exclusion, yet must cooperate on every trip between buildings.

In this report, we first describe the system and the abstractions our model makes of it. We then explain what the controller must guarantee and why, and show how each requirement is made precise, from an informal statement to a formula over the observable actions of the model. Next, we present the model itself, together with the decisions we made along the way and the alternatives we considered. Finally, we describe how we verified the model, including what it took to scale verification beyond the smallest configuration, and reflect on the limitations of our results and how the model could be expanded.


\section{System Description}
\label{sec:system-description}

\subsection{Purpose and Physical Layout}

Touchpoint Towers consists of two wings, A and B, that meet at a single shared location, from now on referred to as \emph{Touchpoint I}. Each wing has five levels and is served by its own elevator, also denoted by \(A\) and \(B\). Level 3 of both wings is Touchpoint I, whereas levels 1, 2, 4 and 5 belong exclusively to their wing. Figure~\ref{fig:layout} shows the layout.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.77\textwidth]{Figures/layout.png}
    \caption{Layout of Touchpoint Towers: rails of elevators \(A\) (orange) and \(B\) (blue) share I (red).}
    \label{fig:layout}
\end{figure}

Touchpoint I is both the intersection of the two elevator rails and the only place where passengers can change wings. The elevators must therefore coordinate their access to it, also when they merely pass through without stopping. A journey within a wing needs one elevator; a journey between wings needs both, with a transfer at I. For example, the journey \(1_A\rightarrow 5_B\) consists of the leg \(1_A\rightarrow I\) on elevator \(A\), followed by the leg \(I\rightarrow 5_B\) on elevator \(B\).

Passengers use the system through a terminal at every floor: they enter their destination, and the terminal's location determines their origin. Each such journey becomes a request to the controller. The controller consists of four components, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}: a dispatcher, the two elevators \(A\) and \(B\), and an intersection manager. A fifth process, the emergency switch, models the building's emergency button.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{Figures/Blank diagram (3).png}
    
    \caption{Architecture of Touchpoint Towers, showing the four principal controller components and the external emergency switch. Solid connections represent regular component interactions, while dotted connections indicate actions restricted by the emergency switch.}

    \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The dispatcher does the bookkeeping. It keeps the pool of outstanding requests, tracks for each whether it is waiting to be picked up or already on board, and tells each elevator which levels it has to visit: hereinafter referred to as its stop set. Note that a request describes a journey, representing one or more passengers making a trip from floor to floor (see Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}). When an elevator serves a level, the dispatcher updates the requests concerned, and reports back once a request is completed.

The elevators move and serve passengers. Each elevator independently follows the LOOK policy~\cite{gfg-look}: it keeps moving in its current direction while stops remain ahead, and reverses only when none do. The elevators never communicate with each other directly. Instead, they coordinate via the dispatcher and the intersection manager.

The intersection manager guards Touchpoint I: an elevator needs its permission before entering or passing through I, and at most one elevator holds this permission at a time. Finally, the emergency switch can pause the system: while an emergency is active, no critical actions are allowed until the system is reset. Elevators do not start new movements, access to I is not granted, and no new requests are accepted.

A journey between wings needs both elevators, in the right order. The dispatcher therefore initially assigns only the first leg, from the passenger's origin to Touchpoint I. Only when the first elevator has delivered the passenger at I is the request rewritten into its second leg, from I to the destination, served by the other elevator. The receiving elevator thus cannot pick up a transferring passenger before they have arrived.

The controller is designed for safe coordination, correct passenger service and eventual completion of every accepted request; optimising waiting times or travel distances is outside the scope of this project.

The model captures the discrete state changes relevant to the safety and liveness requirements, while abstracting from physical details that do not affect the intended verification properties. The following assumptions define the scope of the system.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2.2em,itemsep=0.45\baselineskip]
  \item \textbf{Discrete locations, no time.} Passengers travel between the locations:
  \[
    Loc=\{I,1_A,2_A,4_A,5_A,1_B,2_B,4_B,5_B\}.
  \]
  A movement of an elevator from one level to another is a single atomic step, possibly passing intermediate levels, and cannot be interrupted once started. Travel time, speed and the physical position between levels are not modelled.

  \item \textbf{Passenger Requests.} A request is a journey \((o,d)\) with \(o,d\in Loc\) and \(o\neq d\), and stands for one or more passengers making that journey. A real controller cannot observe who boards or leaves a car without additional hardware or passengers identifying themselves, so the model does not track individual passengers. Passengers making the same journey at the same stage are therefore indistinguishable and complete together.

  \item \textbf{Cooperative Passengers.} When an elevator serves a level, all passengers waiting there for that elevator board, and all passengers in it whose leg ends there leave, instantaneously. Passengers never cancel a request or fail to board.

  \item \textbf{Transfers at I.} A journey between wings consists of two consecutive legs, one per elevator, with a transfer at I. A transferring passenger waits at I until the second elevator picks them up.

  \item \textbf{No capacities.} Elevator cars and floors, including the transfer area at I, can hold any number of passengers.

  \item \textbf{Emergency as a pause.} An emergency pauses the system rather than resetting it: outstanding requests, elevator positions and access to I are preserved, doors are not opened, and service resumes after the reset.

  \item \textbf{Bounded request pool.} At most \texttt{MAXPOOL} requests are outstanding at a time; while the pool is full, further requests are refused. This bound keeps the state space finite. We verify the model for \texttt{MAXPOOL} \(=1\) and \(=2\); Section~\ref{sec:verification} discusses what each bound covers.
\end{enumerate}

Interactions between components are implemented through synchronized actions. The dispatcher and elevators synchronize on \(\mathsf{stops}\) and \(\mathsf{visit}\), while intersection access is coordinated through \(\mathsf{enterI}\) and \(\mathsf{leaveI}\). The emergency switch additionally participates in \(\mathsf{request}\), \(\mathsf{move}\), and \(\mathsf{enterI}\), permitting these actions only when no emergency is active. The actions \(\mathsf{emergency}\) and \(\mathsf{reset}\) are performed by the emergency switch itself, representing external activation and deactivation of the emergency system.

Passenger pickup and dropoff are not represented by separate observable actions. Instead, these operations are reflected in updates to the request pool during \(\mathsf{visit}(e,n)\). The implications of this abstraction for the transfer-order requirement S6 are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:mu-calculus}.

\section{Requirements}
\label{sec:informal-requirements}
\label{sec:action-requirements}

The requirements are divided into safety properties, which state that something bad never happens, and liveness properties, which state that something good eventually happens. Each requirement is stated informally (in italics) and then in terms of the actions of Section~\ref{sec:actions}, with \(e,e'\in\{A,B\}\), \(f_1,f_2,n\in\{1,\ldots,5\}\), \(o,d\in Loc\) and \(S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,5\}\). Section~\ref{sec:mu-calculus} formalises them in the modal \(\mu\)-calculus.

\subsection{Safety Requirements}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{S\arabic*.},leftmargin=3em,itemsep=0.45\baselineskip]
  \item \textit{At most one elevator occupies I at a time.} \\
  Between \(\mathsf{enterI}(e)\) and \(\mathsf{leaveI}(e)\), no \(\mathsf{enterI}(e')\) with \(e'\neq e\) occurs.
  
  \item \textit{An elevator needs permission before entering, leaving or passing through I.} \\
  Every \(\mathsf{move}(e,f_1,f_2)\) with \(\min(f_1,f_2)\leq 3\leq\max(f_1,f_2)\) occurs after an \(\mathsf{enterI}(e)\) and before the next \(\mathsf{leaveI}(e)\).
  
  \item \textit{An elevator never moves with its doors open.} \\
  After \(\mathsf{open}(e)\), no \(\mathsf{move}(e,f_1,f_2)\) occurs before \(\mathsf{close}(e)\).
  
  \item \textit{Passengers are only served with the doors open.} \\
  Every \(\mathsf{visit}(e,n)\) occurs after an \(\mathsf{open}(e)\) and before the next \(\mathsf{close}(e)\).
  
  \item \textit{An elevator only serves levels where it has work.} \\
  A \(\mathsf{visit}(e,n)\) only occurs if the most recent \(\mathsf{stops}(e,S)\) has \(n\in S\).
  
  \item \textit{A passenger changing wings boards the second elevator only after arriving at I with the first.} \\
  This cannot be expressed over the observable actions, since boarding is not an action and requests do not identify passengers. It is guaranteed by construction instead (Section~\ref{sec:mu-calculus}).
 
  \item \textit{A request is only reported completed if it was accepted and is still outstanding.} \\
  Every \(\mathsf{done}(o,d)\) is preceded by more \(\mathsf{request}(o,d)\) than earlier \(\mathsf{done}(o,d)\).
 
  \item \textit{Both elevators stop moving during an emergency.} \\
  After \(\mathsf{emergency}\), no \(\mathsf{move}(e,f_1,f_2)\) occurs before \(\mathsf{reset}\).
  
  \item \textit{An elevator never passes a level where it has work.} \\
  After \(\mathsf{stops}(e,S)\), no \(\mathsf{move}(e,f_1,f_2)\) with a level \(n\in S\) strictly between \(f_1\) and \(f_2\) occurs before the next \(\mathsf{stops}(e,\cdot)\).
  
  \item \textit{Doors only open where the elevator has work.} \\
  \(\mathsf{open}(e)\) only occurs if the level the elevator last moved to (initially level 1) is in its most recent stop set.
\end{enumerate}

Requirement S8 concerns the suspension of elevator movement during an emergency, rather than the complete suspension of all controller operations. Only the critical actions listed in Section~\ref{sec:system-description} are blocked; other operations, such as serving a floor the elevator has already reached, may continue while the emergency is active.

\subsection{Liveness Requirements}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{L\arabic*.},leftmargin=3em,itemsep=0.45\baselineskip]
  \item \textit{The controller never deadlocks.} \\
  In every reachable state, some action is possible.
  
  \item \textit{Every accepted request is eventually completed, during normal operation without further external input.} \\
  After a \(\mathsf{request}(o,d)\) outside an emergency, \(\mathsf{done}(o,d)\) inevitably follows on every path without further \(\mathsf{request}\), \(\mathsf{emergency}\) or \(\mathsf{reset}\).
  
  \item \textit{Every outstanding request can always still be completed.} \\
  After \(\mathsf{request}(o,d)\), as long as no \(\mathsf{done}(o,d)\) has occurred, a \(\mathsf{done}(o,d)\) remains reachable.
  
  \item \textit{No elevator occupies I indefinitely, during normal operation without further external input.} \\
  After \(\mathsf{enterI}(e)\), \(\mathsf{leaveI}(e)\) inevitably follows on every path without further \(\mathsf{request}\), \(\mathsf{emergency}\) or \(\mathsf{reset}\).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Modal \texorpdfstring{$\mu$}{mu}-Calculus Properties}
\label{sec:mu-calculus}

We formalise the requirements of Section~\ref{sec:informal-requirements} in the first-order modal \(\mu\)-calculus with data, using the syntax of the mCRL2 toolset~\cite{groote2014modeling}. Each formula is stored in a separate \texttt{.mcf} file (Appendix~\ref{app:properties}). Section~\ref{subsec:formula-revisions} describes formulas that had to be revised during verification, and Section~\ref{subsec:sanity-checks} the reachability checks that guard against vacuous results.

\subsection{Safety Properties}
\label{subsec:safety-properties}

\paragraph{S1: Mutual exclusion.}
\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
forall e,e2: Car .
  val(e != e2) =>
    [true* . enterI(e) . (!leaveI(e))* . enterI(e2)] false
\end{lstlisting}
The formula prohibits a second elevator from acquiring the intersection while the first still holds it. After \texttt{enterI(e)}, no \texttt{enterI(e2)} with \(e\neq e_2\) may occur before \texttt{leaveI(e)}.

\newpage

\paragraph{S2: Authorized intersection access.}
\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
forall e: Car .
  (
    [(!enterI(e))* .
      (exists f1,f2: Nat .
        val((f1 <= 3 && 3 <= f2) ||
            (f2 <= 3 && 3 <= f1)) &&
        move(e,f1,f2))] false
    &&
    [true* . leaveI(e) . (!enterI(e))* .
      (exists f1,f2: Nat .
        val((f1 <= 3 && 3 <= f2) ||
            (f2 <= 3 && 3 <= f1)) &&
        move(e,f1,f2))] false
  )
\end{lstlisting}
All of entering, leaving or crossing I must lie between an \texttt{enterI(e)} and the next \texttt{leaveI(e)}: the first part covers moves before the first acquisition, the second moves after a release (see Section~\ref{subsec:formula-revisions} for quantifier).

\paragraph{S3: Movement with closed doors.}
\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
forall e: Car .
  [true* . open(e) . (!close(e))* .
    (exists f1,f2: Nat . move(e,f1,f2))] false
\end{lstlisting}
The formula prohibits movement after an elevator opens its doors and before it closes them again. This ensures that movement occurs only with closed doors, assuming both elevators are initially in that state.

\paragraph{S4: Service with open doors.}
\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
forall e: Car .
  (
    [(!open(e))* .
      (exists n: Nat . visit(e,n))] false
    &&
    [true* . close(e) . (!open(e))* .
      (exists n: Nat . visit(e,n))] false
  )
\end{lstlisting}
The first part prohibits service before the initial door opening, while the second prohibits service after closing until the next opening. Thus, every \texttt{visit(e,n)} must occur while the elevator doors are open. 

\paragraph{S5: Service only at required stops.}
\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
forall e: Car .
forall n: Nat .
  val(n >= 1 && n <= 5) =>
  (
    [(!(exists S: FSet(Nat) . stops(e,S)))* .
      visit(e,n)] false
    &&
    [true* .
      (exists S: FSet(Nat) .
        stops(e,S) && val(!(n in S))) .
      (!(exists T: FSet(Nat) . stops(e,T)))* .
      visit(e,n)] false
  )
\end{lstlisting}
The formula ensures that an elevator serves only levels contained in its most recently communicated stop set. The first part prohibits service before receiving any stop set, while the second prohibits visiting a level excluded from the latest stop set until another update occurs.

\paragraph{S6: Correct passenger transfer order.}
S6 is not specified as a formula. As argued in Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}, the model tracks journeys rather than individual passengers, and boarding is not an observable action, so the requirement cannot be expressed over the actions of our model. Instead, it holds by construction: the dispatcher only creates the second leg of a journey once the first elevator has delivered the passengers at I, as shown in Section~\ref{subsec:cross-wing}. Sanity check R7 confirms that journeys between wings can indeed be completed.

\paragraph{S7: No phantom completions.}
\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
forall o, d: Loc .
  val(validLoc(o) && validLoc(d)) =>
    nu X(k: Nat = 0) .
    (
      [request(o,d)]
        X(min(k + 1, MAXPOOL))
      &&
      [done(o,d)]
        (val(k > 0) &&
         X(if(k > 0, Int2Nat(k - 1), 0)))
      &&
      [!(request(o,d) || done(o,d))]
        X(k)
    )
\end{lstlisting}
The formula keeps a counter \(k\) of outstanding requests for each journey \((o,d)\). A request increases \(k\), and a completion is only allowed if \(k>0\), after which it decreases \(k\). To keep the formula finite, \(k\) is capped at \texttt{MAXPOOL}. This cannot hide a violation, because the capped counter never exceeds the real number of outstanding requests, and it cannot cause a false alarm, because at most \texttt{MAXPOOL} completions of \((o,d)\) can be pending at once. Section~\ref{subsec:formula-revisions} explains why this revision was needed.

\paragraph{S8: Emergency suspension.}
\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
forall e: Car .
  [true* . emergency . (!reset)* .
    (exists f1,f2: Nat . move(e,f1,f2))] false
\end{lstlisting}
The formula prohibits movement by either elevator after emergency activation and before the subsequent reset. It does not prohibit other controller actions, consistent with the emergency semantics described in Section~\ref{sec:assumptions}.

\paragraph{S9: No skipped required stops.}

\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
forall e: Car .
forall n: Nat .
  val(n >= 1 && n <= 5) =>
    [true* .
      (exists S: FSet(Nat) .
        stops(e,S) && val(n in S)) .
      (!(exists T: FSet(Nat) . stops(e,T)))* .
      (exists f1,f2: Nat .
        move(e,f1,f2) &&
        val((f1 < n && n < f2) ||
            (f2 < n && n < f1)))] false
\end{lstlisting}
After receiving a stop set containing level \(n\), the elevator may not move past that level without first updating its stop set. The formula covers movement in both directions and prevents required intermediate stops from being skipped. As explained in Section~\ref{subsec:sanity-checks}, the relevant multi-stop scenario becomes reachable with \texttt{MAXPOOL = 2}.

\paragraph{S10: Door opening only for service.}
\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
forall e: Car .
  nu X(pos: Nat = 1, S: FSet(Nat) = {}) .
  (
    (forall T: FSet(Nat) .
      [stops(e,T)] X(pos,T))
    &&
    (forall f1,f2: Nat .
      [move(e,f1,f2)] X(f2,S))
    &&
    [!((exists T: FSet(Nat) . stops(e,T)) ||
       (exists f1,f2: Nat . move(e,f1,f2)))]
      X(pos,S)
    &&
    [open(e)] val(pos in S)
  )
\end{lstlisting}

The greatest fixed point tracks the elevator's current position \texttt{pos} and its most recently communicated stop set \texttt{S}. Receiving a new stop set updates \texttt{S}, while movement updates \texttt{pos}. All other actions preserve these values. Door opening is allowed only when the current position belongs to the stored stop set, with both elevators initially positioned at level 1.

\subsection{Liveness Properties}
\label{subsec:liveness-properties}

The liveness properties concern the ability of the controller to make progress. In particular, they distinguish between the possibility of completing a request and its inevitable completion under uninterrupted normal operation.

\paragraph{L1: Deadlock freedom.}
\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
[true*]<true>true
\end{lstlisting}
The formula requires every reachable state to have at least one enabled transition. This excludes deadlocks but does not by itself guarantee progress towards completing outstanding requests.

\newpage

\paragraph{L2: Eventual request completion.}
\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
nu M(normal: Bool = true) .
(
  [emergency] M(false)
  &&
  [reset] M(true)
  &&
  (forall o,d: Loc .
    [request(o,d)]
      (
        M(normal)
        &&
        (
          val(!normal)
          ||
          mu X .
          (
            [!(done(o,d) ||
               (exists x,y: Loc . request(x,y)) ||
               emergency || reset)] X
            &&
            <!( (exists x,y: Loc . request(x,y)) ||
                 emergency || reset)> true
          )
        )
      )
  )
  &&
  [!(emergency || reset ||
     (exists x,y: Loc . request(x,y)))]
    M(normal)
)
\end{lstlisting}
The outer greatest fixed point follows every run of the system and remembers in \texttt{normal} whether an emergency is active. After each request made outside an emergency, the inner least fixed point demands that \texttt{done(o,d)} is reached within finitely many steps on every path without further requests, emergencies or resets; its diamond part ensures that the system does not get stuck before that. These exclusions are needed because the environment is not fair: it could otherwise keep adding requests or triggering emergencies forever, and no controller could guarantee completion. Requests during an emergency are impossible anyway, as the emergency switch blocks them; the \texttt{normal} flag simply makes this case explicit.

\paragraph{L3: Persistent serviceability.}
\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
forall o, d: Loc .
  val(validLoc(o) && validLoc(d)) =>
    [true* . request(o,d) . (!done(o,d))*]
      <true* . done(o,d)> true
\end{lstlisting}
The formula states that after a request, as long as it has not been completed, the system can always still complete it. Unlike L2, it only demands that completion remains possible, not that it actually happens on every run. The quantification is restricted to valid locations, as discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:formula-revisions}.

\paragraph{L4: Eventual intersection release.}
\noindent \begin{lstlisting}
forall e: Car .
  [true* . enterI(e)]
    mu X .
    (
      [!(leaveI(e) ||
         (exists o,d: Loc . request(o,d)) ||
         emergency || reset)] X
      &&
      <!( (exists o,d: Loc . request(o,d)) ||
           emergency || reset)> true
    )
\end{lstlisting}

After an elevator enters I, the least fixed point demands that it leaves I within finitely many steps on every path without further requests, emergencies or resets. As in L2, the diamond part rules out getting stuck before that, so an elevator can hold I neither forever through a deadlock nor through an endless loop of other actions.


\subsection{Formula Revisions}
\label{subsec:formula-revisions}
%%% GUIDE (new subsection): shows the verification process, worth keeping. Source: docs/design.md section 5.
%%% - S2: the first version quantified `forall f1, f2: Nat` OUTSIDE the modal operators. The solver then has to check
%%%   infinitely many pairs and never terminates. Moving the quantifier inside the action formula (exists ... && move)
%%%   matches only the moves that actually occur. Same meaning.
%%% - S7: two unbounded counters -> infinitely many fixpoint instances. Replaced by one capped counter (soundness argument).
%%% - S7, L3: `forall o, d: Loc` ranges over infinitely many locations (fl(A,17), ...). Bounded with validLoc;
%%%   not a weakening because request/done only occur with valid locations.
%%% - General lesson: quantifiers over infinite sorts at the top of a formula must be bounded or moved into the action.
%%% - Not a formula change, but related: at MAXPOOL = 2, S7, L3 and L2 are checked once per trip (o, d): 72 instances
%%%   whose conjunction is the original formula (see Scaling to MAXPOOL = 2). For S7/L3 the validLoc bound becomes
%%%   `o == P && d == Q`; for L2 only the obligation guard becomes `val(!normal || !(o == P && d == Q))`, because
%%%   restricting L2's outer forall would stop following runs after other requests and weaken the formula.

During verification, several formulas required revisions to avoid infinite unfolding caused by quantification over unbounded data types. These changes preserve the intended requirements while making their evaluation feasible.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Quantifiers over infinite sorts.} S2 first quantified \texttt{forall f1,f2: Nat} in front of the whole formula, and S7 and L3 quantified \texttt{forall o,d: Loc}, where \texttt{Loc} also contains invalid locations such as \texttt{fl(A,17)}. The solver instantiates such a quantifier for every value, of which there are infinitely many. In S2, we moved the quantifier into the action formula, so that it only ranges over moves that actually occur; in S7 and L3, we restricted it with \texttt{validLoc}. Neither change alters the meaning, since every move is still covered and requests and completions only involve valid locations.
  \item \textbf{Unbounded fixed-point parameters.} S7 first counted accepted and completed requests separately. Since requests can be made forever, both counters grow without bound, and every combination of values yields a separate equation. A single counter of outstanding requests, capped at \texttt{MAXPOOL}, suffices.
\end{itemize}

In short, every quantifier and fixed-point parameter must range over finitely many relevant values, or verification may not terminate. At \texttt{MAXPOOL = 2}, S7, L2 and L3 are additionally checked as one instance per origin; this changes how they are checked, not what they mean, and is described in Section~\ref{subsec:scaling}.

\section{Behavioural Specification in mCRL2}
\label{sec:behavioural-specification}
%%% GUIDE: an engineer must be able to rebuild the controller from this section. Per component: state (process
%%%   parameters), what it can do in each state (the + branches), and how each action changes the state.
%%%   Show key snippets; the full spec goes in the appendix. Decisions worth including are listed per subsection.

The controller is specified as five parallel processes: a dispatcher, two elevators, an intersection manager, and an emergency switch. Each process maintains its own state and communicates through synchronized actions. This section describes the data structures, process behaviour, and design decisions underlying the specification. The complete mCRL2 model is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:spec}.


\subsection{Data}

\label{subsec:data}
%%% - Loc = I | fl(wing, lvl); validLoc needed because Nat is infinite (fl(A,17) is a legal term).
%%% - Request = req(origSrc, origDst, src, dst, st), st in {waiting, active, finished}: original trip + current leg.
%%% - Pool = List(Req) kept SORTED and DUPLICATE-FREE by ins. Why not FSet: mCRL2 cannot pattern-match on FSet, so
%%%   recursive functions over it (stop sets, visit updates) cannot be written; we tested this.
%%%   Why sorted: [x,y] and [y,x] would otherwise be different states for the same pool (state-space blow-up);
%%%   sorting uses the < that mCRL2 generates for every struct.
%%% - Stop sets stay FSet(Nat): they only need membership tests.
%%% - Conditional equations for nextUp/nextDown: an if() version made mcrl22lps loop forever, because during
%%%   linearisation the position is still a variable and the rewriter unfolds the recursion endlessly; a conditional
%%%   equation only fires once its condition is decidable. Demonstrated with mcrl2i. (Source: docs/questions.md Q7.)

Elevators are identified by the type \texttt{Car}, and passenger locations by \texttt{Loc}: a location is either Touchpoint \texttt{I} or a floor \texttt{fl(w,n)} of wing \texttt{w}. As floor numbers are natural numbers, \texttt{Loc} also contains invalid floors such as \texttt{fl(A,17)}; the predicate \texttt{validLoc} restricts requests to levels 1, 2, 4 and 5 of either wing and I. \\

\begin{lstlisting}
sort Car = struct A | B;
     Loc = struct I | fl(wing: Car, lvl: Nat);
     State = struct waiting | active | finished;
     Req = struct req(origSrc: Loc, origDst: Loc,
                      src: Loc, dst: Loc, st: State);
     Pool = List(Req);
\end{lstlisting}

Each request stores five fields: the original journey (\texttt{origSrc}, \texttt{origDst}), the current leg (\texttt{src}, \texttt{dst}) and its state. A new request is \texttt{waiting}, becomes \texttt{active} when its origin is served, and becomes \texttt{finished} when its final destination is reached. Storing the original journey next to the current leg is what keeps journeys between wings simple: a single entry can be rewritten from its first into its second leg at I, while \texttt{done} still reports the journey that was originally requested. The alternative, two separate entries per journey, would need extra bookkeeping to link the legs; Section~\ref{subsec:cross-wing} discusses this choice.

The dispatcher stores outstanding requests in a \texttt{List(Req)} rather than a finite set. This choice allows recursive functions to inspect and update individual requests through list pattern matching, which is not supported in the same way for \texttt{FSet} in mCRL2. To avoid representing equivalent request pools in different orders, the insertion function \texttt{ins} maintains a sorted, duplicate-free list using the ordering defined for structured data types.

\begin{lstlisting}
ins(r, []) = [r];
ins(r, q |> p) =
  if(r == q, q |> p,
    if(r < q, r |> q |> p, q |> ins(r,p)));
\end{lstlisting}

Without this canonical ordering, pools containing the same requests in different insertion orders would correspond to distinct states, unnecessarily increasing the state space. Identical entries are also merged regardless of their processing state, provided all five stored fields match. Unlike the request pool, elevator stop sets use \texttt{FSet(Nat)}, since they require only membership tests and set operations rather than recursive updates of individual records.

The model also defines helper functions for determining request ownership, extracting stop sets, updating requests after floor visits, and selecting elevator targets. In particular, the LOOK scheduling functions \texttt{nextUp} and \texttt{nextDown} search for the nearest required stop above or below the current level. These functions use conditional equations rather than recursively nested \texttt{if} expressions, as illustrated below.

\begin{lstlisting}
n >= 5 -> nextUp(n, S) = 0;
n < 5 -> nextUp(n, S) =
  if(n + 1 in S, n + 1, nextUp(n + 1, S));
\end{lstlisting}

An earlier implementation using an unconditional equation with an \texttt{if} expression caused linearization to unfold the recursive calls indefinitely when the elevator position was still symbolic. Conditional equations avoid this issue because recursion proceeds only when the corresponding condition can be evaluated. This behaviour was investigated using \texttt{mcrl2i} before adopting the revised definitions. Together, these data structures and helper functions support request management and scheduling while limiting unnecessary state-space growth.

\subsection{Dispatcher}
\label{subsec:dispatcher}
%%% - State: the pool. Branches: accept request (guard: valid, o != d, pool not full); offer car e its stop set
%%%   stopsOf(e, pool); process visit(e, n) via afterVisit; report done for a finished request.
%%% - NO PHASES: our first idea was a cycle "bookkeeping -> send stopsA -> send stopsB -> wait". With blocking
%%%   handshakes this deadlocks (dispatcher waits for car B to take its stops, car B waits for the dispatcher to accept
%%%   its visit). Instead all branches are always offered; bookkeeping is the new parameter value of the transition.
%%% - Stop set = origins of waiting entries + current-leg destinations of active entries; finished contributes nothing.
%%% - afterVisit: board (waiting -> active), drop off (active -> finished, or rewritten into leg 2 at I), keep others;
%%%   re-insert with ins (a changed entry may move in the order or merge with an identical one).
%%% - done branch: a + branch, not a clean-up mode (a mode would block requests and visits); sums over o, d: Loc with
%%%   validLoc guards because that pattern is known to enumerate. Why done at all: design.md 6.2.

The dispatcher maintains the pool of outstanding requests and coordinates passenger service through four alternative actions: accepting new requests, communicating required stop sets, processing floor visits, and reporting completed journeys. Its only process parameter is \texttt{pool}, which is updated whenever a request is accepted, a floor is served, or a completed request is removed.

\begin{lstlisting}
proc Dispatcher(pool: Pool) =
  sum o, d: Loc .
    (validLoc(o) && validLoc(d) && o != d
      && #pool < MAXPOOL)
    -> request_d(o, d) .
       Dispatcher(ins(newReq(o, d), pool))
  + sum e: Car .
      stops_d(e, stopsOf(e, pool)) .
      Dispatcher(pool)
  + sum e: Car, n: Nat .
      visit_d(e, n) .
      Dispatcher(afterVisit(e, n, pool))
  + sum o, d: Loc .
      (validLoc(o) && validLoc(d) &&
       hasFinished(o, d, pool))
    -> done(o, d) .
       Dispatcher(removeFinished(o, d, pool));
\end{lstlisting}

A new request is accepted only if both locations are valid, the origin differs from the destination, and the pool has not reached \texttt{MAXPOOL}. The function \texttt{newReq} initializes the first transportation leg, while \texttt{ins} inserts it into the sorted pool. The dispatcher communicates stop sets through \texttt{stopsOf}, which collects the origins of waiting requests and the destinations of active requests belonging to each elevator. Finished requests contribute no stops.

When an elevator performs \texttt{visit(e,n)}, the function \texttt{afterVisit} updates all applicable requests in the pool. Waiting requests originating at level \texttt{n} become active, while active requests with that destination are either marked as finished or rewritten into their second leg at I. Unaffected requests retain their state. Each updated entry is reinserted using \texttt{ins}, preserving the canonical ordering and merging identical entries when applicable. The separate \texttt{done} action reports completion of the original journey and removes the corresponding finished entry.

An earlier design organized dispatching into sequential phases, such as sending stop sets to both elevators before accepting further updates. However, blocking synchronization could produce deadlocks when one elevator waited for the dispatcher to process a visit while the dispatcher waited for the other elevator to receive its stop set. The final specification therefore offers all four alternatives continuously, allowing whichever synchronization is enabled to proceed. Request bookkeeping is represented directly by changes to the process parameter rather than by separate controller phases. Similarly, completed requests are removed through an ordinary alternative instead of a dedicated cleanup phase that could temporarily block other interactions.

\subsection{Elevators}
\label{subsec:elevators}
%%% - State: e, pos, dir. Three phases as separate processes (Elevator: read stops + choose target; Go: move with the
%%%   intersection protocol; Serve: open, visit, close). mCRL2 merges them into one state machine.
%%% - Reading the stop set: sum S . (S != {} || pos == 3) -> stops_c(e, S). The guard makes an idle car WAIT instead of
%%%   polling; polling an empty stop set forever would be a livelock that breaks the "inevitably" in L2/L4.
%%% - LOOK: target = current level if it has work, else nearest stop ahead, else nearest behind (reverse).
%%%   Serving the current floor is allowed; forbidding it starves a lone request at the car's floor. No livelock:
%%%   a visit always removes that level from the stop set.
%%% - Intersection protocol, from the observation "a car holds I exactly when it is at level 3 (or inside its own
%%%   crossing move)": no 3 -> move; to 3 -> enterI.move; from 3 -> move.leaveI; through 3 -> enterI.move.leaveI.
%%%   The if-else chain was checked strongly bisimilar to a two-question version (needEnter / needLeave) with ltscompare.
%%% - Idle at I: with no work, a car at 3 reads {} and moves one level on in its direction, then leaves I, without
%%%   serving (opening the doors there would violate S10). Without this rule an idle car blocks I forever (L4).
%%% - Pitfall worth a sentence: writing the work and idle branches as two separate stops_c branches made the
%%%   linearizer cluster them, after which sum elimination could no longer remove `sum S: FSet(Nat)` and state space
%%%   generation failed ("cannot enumerate FSet(Nat)"). One branch with an inner if-then-else fixed it.

Both elevators use the same parameterized process \texttt{Elevator(e,pos,dir)}, where \texttt{e} identifies the elevator, \texttt{pos} stores its current level, and \texttt{dir} records its travel direction. Elevator behaviour is divided into three processes: \texttt{Elevator} obtains the stop set and selects a destination, \texttt{Go} performs the required movement and intersection synchronization, and \texttt{Serve} handles passenger service.

\begin{lstlisting}
proc Elevator(e: Car, pos: Nat, dir: Dir) =
  sum S: FSet(Nat) .
    (S != {} || pos == 3) -> stops_c(e, S) .
    (
      (S != {}) ->
        Go(e, pos, newDir(pos, dir, S),
           target(pos, dir, S))
      <>
        move_c(e, pos, moveOne(pos, dir)) .
        leaveI_c(e) .
        Elevator(e, moveOne(pos, dir), dir)
    );
\end{lstlisting}

Each elevator follows LOOK scheduling: it first serves its current level if required, otherwise selects the nearest stop in its current direction, reversing only when no required stops remain ahead. Allowing service at the current position is essential, since a request originating there might otherwise remain unserved when no other stops exist. After service, the stop set is recalculated, preventing repeated service of a level that no longer requires attention.

The guard \texttt{S != \{\} || pos == 3} prevents an idle elevator outside I from repeatedly requesting empty stop sets. Without this restriction, infinite polling could occur without useful progress, violating the eventuality requirements L2 and L4. An exception is necessary at I: if an elevator has no outstanding work while occupying the interchange, it reads the empty stop set, and moves away from the intersection (making a move in its current direction), and yields the mutex. It does so without opening its doors, preserving S10 while preventing idling at the intersection. 

Movement is performed by \texttt{Go}, which distinguishes four cases according to whether the chosen movement involves the intersection. Movements entirely outside I require no additional synchronization. An elevator approaching I must first acquire the intersection; one leaving I releases it after movement; and one passing through I must acquire and subsequently release it.

\begin{lstlisting}
proc Go(e: Car, pos: Nat, dir: Dir, t: Nat) =
  (t == pos) -> Serve(e, pos, dir)
  <>
  (!crossIntersection(pos, t)) ->
    move_c(e, pos, t) .
    Serve(e, t, dir)
  <>
  (t == 3) ->
    enterI_c(e) .
    move_c(e, pos, t) .
    Serve(e, t, dir)
  <>
  (pos == 3) ->
    move_c(e, pos, t) .
    leaveI_c(e) .
    Serve(e, t, dir)
  <>
    enterI_c(e) .
    move_c(e, pos, t) .
    leaveI_c(e) .
    Serve(e, t, dir);
\end{lstlisting}

This protocol ensures that movement involving I is synchronized with the intersection manager, while ordinary movements remain independent of it. During development, this case-based implementation was also compared with an alternative formulation based on separate acquisition and release conditions. Strong bisimulation checking with \texttt{ltscompare} confirmed their behavioural equivalence.

\newpage

\begin{lstlisting}
proc Serve(e: Car, pos: Nat, dir: Dir) =
  open(e) .
  visit_c(e, pos) .
  close(e) .
  Elevator(e, pos, dir);
\end{lstlisting}

Passenger service follows a fixed sequence of opening the doors, synchronizing the floor visit with the dispatcher, and closing the doors before returning to scheduling. This structure enforces the required relationship between door operations and service. During implementation, the working and idle alternatives were also consolidated under a single \texttt{stops\_c} branch, because separate branches prevented successful elimination of a symbolic \texttt{FSet(Nat)} parameter during linearization.

\subsection{Intersection Manager}
\label{subsec:intersection-manager}
%%% - State: free | heldBy(e). Free: any car may enterI. Held by e: only e may leaveI. Two lines of mCRL2.
%%% - The dispatcher and the intersection manager do not need to communicate.
%%% - Mention: no fairness between the cars for access to I (A could in principle always win); not required by S1-L4.

The intersection manager enforces mutual exclusion at Touchpoint I through a single process parameter \texttt{mutex}, whose value is either \texttt{free} or \texttt{heldBy(e)} for one of the elevators. When the intersection is free, either elevator may acquire it through \texttt{enterI}. Once ownership has been granted, only the owning elevator can release it through \texttt{leaveI}.

\begin{lstlisting}
sort Mutex = struct free | heldBy(holder: Car);

proc IntersectionManager(mutex: Mutex) =
  (mutex == free) ->
    sum e: Car .
      enterI_im(e) .
      IntersectionManager(heldBy(e))
  <>
    leaveI_im(holder(mutex)) .
    IntersectionManager(free);
\end{lstlisting}

The manager does not directly control elevator movements. Instead, the synchronization requirements in \texttt{Go} ensure that an elevator must acquire the intersection before entering or crossing it and release ownership after leaving. The manager itself only records which elevator currently holds permission. This separation keeps the mutual-exclusion mechanism independent of the dispatcher's request management.

No direct communication is required between the dispatcher and the intersection manager, since each elevator independently coordinates its work with the dispatcher and its intersection access with the manager. The protocol guarantees exclusive access but does not impose fairness when both elevators request ownership. Such scheduling guarantees are outside the current requirements; the liveness properties instead concern progress under the stated restrictions on external activity.

    \subsection{Emergency Switch}
\label{subsec:emergency-switch}
%%% - Design goal: an emergency PAUSES the system, it does not reset it; otherwise requests would be lost and
%%%   "every request is eventually served" would be false.
%%% - The switch is a separate process (the button): on/off, and it must take part in every move, request and
%%%   enterI, which it only allows while off. Everything else (stop-set reads, visits, done) is bookkeeping and continues.
%%% - Moves are atomic, so there is no "mid-move" to interrupt: no new move starts.
%%% - enterI gating was found by reviewing L4 before running it: without it, a car could acquire I during an emergency
%%%   and then be unable to move, so L4's "leaveI inevitably follows" fails. Good example of model review.
%%% - Compared to the first design (both cars synchronise on emergency/reset directly), the cars barely change.

The emergency switch is modelled as a separate process with a Boolean parameter \texttt{on}, indicating whether an emergency is active. Its purpose is to pause the system rather than reset it, preserving elevator positions, outstanding requests, and intersection ownership so that service can resume after the emergency is cleared. This is implemented by requiring the switch to synchronize with every \texttt{move}, \texttt{request}, and \texttt{enterI} action, which it permits only while \texttt{on} is false.

\begin{lstlisting}
proc EmergencySwitch(on: Bool) =
    (!on) -> emergency . EmergencySwitch(true)
  + (on) -> reset . EmergencySwitch(false)
  + (!on) -> sum e: Car, f1, f2: Nat .
      move_em(e, f1, f2) . EmergencySwitch(on)
  + (!on) -> sum o, d: Loc .
      request_em(o, d) . EmergencySwitch(on)
  + (!on) -> sum e: Car .
      enterI_em(e) . EmergencySwitch(on);
\end{lstlisting}

During an emergency, no new movements, passenger requests, or intersection acquisitions can occur. However, other operations, including stop-set communication, floor visits, door operations, and completion reporting, remain possible. An elevator already serving a floor may therefore finish its service sequence. Since movements are atomic, the model does not represent interruptions halfway through a movement; instead, the emergency prevents subsequent movements from starting. No state is discarded, and normal operation resumes after \texttt{reset}.

An important refinement concerned the synchronization of \texttt{enterI}. Initially, the switch restricted only movements and requests. However, reviewing requirement L4 revealed that an elevator could acquire the intersection during an emergency and subsequently be unable to move away, preventing eventual release. The final design therefore also synchronizes \texttt{enterI} with the emergency switch. Removing this restriction caused the model to violate L4. 


\subsection{Cross-wing Requests}
\label{subsec:cross-wing}
%%% THE most interesting design story; source: docs/design.md section 6.1. Structure suggestion:
%%% - Starting point: working same-wing model with (src, dst, state) triples; goal: extend with minimal disruption.
%%% - What leg 2 needs: neither waiting (B would go to I too early) nor active (B would drop off without pickup).
%%% - First idea "when A visits I, unblock all of B's blocked legs" is wrong: releases passengers who are not at I yet.
%%% - Root cause: a triple cannot say which leg belongs to which trip.
%%% - Option 1 (two linked entries, blocked leg 2) and its merge trap (a second identical request strands a passenger).
%%% - Option 2 (one entry, rewritten at I): order by construction, no blocked state, no stranding, one pool slot per
%%%   request, owner/stopsOf unchanged. CHOSEN.
%%% - One vs two extra fields: same state space (the extra field is determined by the others); two chosen for
%%%   readability. Why the original origin is needed at all: done must report the original trip.
%%% - Walkthrough table 1A -> 4B (design.md 6.1).

Supporting cross-wing journeys required extending the initial model, which handled only same-wing requests represented by an origin, destination, and processing state. A cross-wing journey consists of two consecutive legs, with the passenger first travelling to I and then continuing on the other elevator. The main challenge was ensuring that the second leg could not begin before the passenger arrived at the interchange, while preserving the existing dispatcher and scheduling mechanisms.

The two original request states, \texttt{waiting} and \texttt{active}, were insufficient to represent a second leg stored in advance. Marking it as waiting would allow the receiving elevator to travel to I before the passenger arrived, while marking it as active would allow the elevator to serve the destination without performing the pickup. An initial approach therefore stored both legs separately, with the second leg blocked until the first was completed. This introduced a problem when multiple passengers submitted identical journeys: since \texttt{ins} merges identical pool entries, two requests could have distinct first legs but share a single blocked second leg, leaving the later passenger without a transfer. Simply unblocking all second legs whenever an elevator visited I would be incorrect as well, since such a visit does not imply that every transferring passenger has arrived. Correctly linking the two legs would therefore require additional bookkeeping.

The final solution represents each journey by a single request entry that is rewritten when the first leg is completed. Alongside the current leg and its state, the entry preserves the original origin and destination, using the structure \texttt{req(origSrc,origDst,src,dst,st)}. A new cross-wing request initially targets I:

\begin{lstlisting}
newReq(x, y) =
  req(x, y, x, if(sameWing(x,y), y, I), waiting);
\end{lstlisting}

When the first elevator serves I, \texttt{afterVisit} replaces the active first leg with a waiting second leg from I to the original destination, as Table~\ref{tab:cross-wing-example} illustrates for a journey from \(1_A\) to \(4_B\). The ownership and stop-set functions continue to operate on the current leg, without special rules for transfers.

\toprule

This guarantees the transfer order, and thus requirement S6, by construction: the second leg does not exist until the first has been completed, so the receiving elevator cannot see the transfer in its stop set before the passengers have arrived at I. No blocked state is needed, and requests at different stages remain distinguishable, while identical requests that reach the same stage merge safely and are served together. We store both original endpoints, rather than only the origin with the current destination recomputed when needed, for readability; the extra field is determined by the other fields and therefore adds no states. Preserving the original endpoints is essential in either case, since \texttt{done(o,d)} must report the journey that was accepted rather than its final leg.


\subsection{Composition}
\label{subsec:composition}
%%% - The init: five processes in parallel, comm rules (list them), allow set (the visible actions of the table).
%%% - Why comm + allow: halves have different names from the result, so allow can drop a half that happens alone and
%%%   any unwanted multi-action; the result keeps a name and data, unlike tau in CCS.

The five processes run in parallel and interact only through synchronised actions. Each synchronisation consists of halves suffixed by component (\texttt{\_d} dispatcher, \texttt{\_c} elevator, \texttt{\_im} intersection manager, \texttt{\_em} emergency switch), which \texttt{comm} fuses into a single action without a suffix.

\begin{lstlisting}
init allow({request, stops, visit, move, open, close,
            enterI, leaveI, done, emergency, reset},
  comm({stops_d | stops_c -> stops,
        visit_d | visit_c -> visit,
        enterI_c | enterI_im | enterI_em -> enterI,
        leaveI_c | leaveI_im -> leaveI,
        move_c | move_em -> move,
        request_d | request_em -> request},
    Dispatcher([]) || Elevator(A, 1, up) || Elevator(B, 1, up)
    || IntersectionManager(free) || EmergencySwitch(false)));
\end{lstlisting}

Since \texttt{allow} only admits the fused actions, a half can never occur on its own; this is how the model enforces handshakes. For example, an elevator cannot move without the emergency switch, nor enter I without both the intersection manager and the switch. Unlike hiding, the fused actions keep their name and data, so the requirements can refer to them directly. Initially, the pool is empty, both elevators are at level 1 heading up, I is free and no emergency is active.


\section{Verification}
\label{sec:verification}

We verified the model for two bounds on the request pool: \texttt{MAXPOOL = 1} on its complete state space, and \texttt{MAXPOOL = 2} using a per-property abstraction. All requirements hold at both bounds.

\subsection{Setup and Reproducibility}
\label{subsec:setup}

We used mCRL2 202607.0 (Release) on an HP ZBook Power G9 laptop (Intel Core i7-12700H, 16\,GB RAM, Windows 11 Enterprise 10.0.26200).\footnote{Windows tends to suspend idle laptops during long runs. To keep the system active during the \texttt{MAXPOOL = 2} verification, which took over six hours, an autoclicker played Cookie Clicker in the background.} The state space is generated once, reduced modulo strong bisimulation, which preserves all \(\mu\)-calculus properties, and every property is checked against the result:

\begin{lstlisting}
mcrl22lps spec.mcrl2 model.lps
lpssumelm model.lps model.lps
lpsconstelm model.lps model.lps
lpsparelm model.lps model.lps
lps2lts --threads=4 model.lps model.lts
ltsconvert -ebisim model.lts model_min.lts
lts2pbes -l model.lps -f S1.mcf model_min.lts S1.pbes
pbessolve S1.pbes
\end{lstlisting}

This is much faster than checking in the mCRL2 IDE, which re-evaluates all data functions for every formula (S1: 10\,s instead of 81\,s). The scripts in Appendix~\ref{app:scripts} contain all commands.

\subsection{Scaling to MAXPOOL = 2}
\label{subsec:scaling}

\texttt{MAXPOOL = 2} matters because many scenarios need two outstanding requests, such as merging requests, stop sets that change while an elevator is under way, or both elevators being busy at once; at \texttt{MAXPOOL = 1}, S9 is even vacuous (Section~\ref{subsec:sanity-checks}). However, checking the 18.8 million states ran out of memory. We ruled out a modelling error (all states are genuine) and non-terminating formulas, and measured that plain checking would need 30--50\,GB, two to three times the memory of the laptop.

We therefore used a \emph{per-property abstraction} (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). For each group of properties, we hide all actions their formulas do not mention, turning them into internal \(\tau\) steps, minimise modulo branching bisimulation (\texttt{ltsconvert --tau=...\ -ebranching-bisim}), and check the formulas on the result. This preserves every formula that is insensitive to inserting or removing internal steps, which holds for ours, as they always place a \texttt{*} between actions. For L1, L2 and L4, infinite internal loops matter, so their groups use divergence-preserving branching bisimulation. Only R4 and R9, which require two actions directly after each other, are not preserved. We validated the approach at \texttt{MAXPOOL = 1}: all properties give the same results as on the full state space, and a deliberately faulty model, without the emergency gating of \texttt{enterI}, still violates L4.

    \caption{Verification pipeline: at \texttt{MAXPOOL = 1}, properties are checked on the full state space; at \texttt{MAXPOOL = 2}, on per-group abstractions.}
    \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

S7, L2 and L3 range over all 72 journeys, which made even their abstractions too large to check at once. Since such a formula holds if and only if it holds for the journeys from each origin, we checked it as nine instances, one per origin \(P\). For S7 and L3, \texttt{validLoc(o)} becomes \texttt{o == P}. For L2, only the completion obligation is restricted to requests from \(P\); the outer fixed point must keep following all requests, as restricting it would weaken the formula.

The sanity checks show that the properties are not satisfied vacuously, with one expected exception. \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Design Decisions}
\label{subsec:design-decisions}

We built the model in levels of increasing complexity: first the dispatcher with one elevator, then the second elevator with the intersection manager, then cross-wing journeys and completion reporting, and finally the emergency switch. Each data function was tested with \texttt{mcrl2i} before it was used in a process, which exposed problems such as the non-terminating \texttt{if}-based recursion early. Several decisions favored the simplest structure that made a problem impossible rather than a rule that prevents it: e.g. a journey that is rewritten at I cannot start its second leg too early. In the words of Hoare~\cite{hoare1981emperor}, a design can be made ``so simple that there are obviously no deficiencies'' or ``so complicated that there are no obvious deficiencies'', and ``the first method is far more difficult''; we found this to be true.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bounded verification.} We verified at most two outstanding requests; behaviour that only arises with three or more is not covered.
  \item \textbf{Abstraction.} The results for \texttt{MAXPOOL = 2} rely on the per-property abstraction preserving our formulas. We argued this and validated it at \texttt{MAXPOOL = 1}, but did not prove it.
  \item \textbf{Journeys, not passengers.} S6 is argued by construction rather than model-checked, and a single completion may cover several passengers making the same journey.
  \item \textbf{Liveness assumptions.} L2 and L4 only hold on runs without further requests or emergencies, and there is no fairness between the elevators for access to I. Under continuous traffic, service is possible (L3) but not guaranteed.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{subsec:future-work}

A natural next step is to limit the number of passengers who can wait at I, which we expect to introduce new deadlocks between the two elevators. Modelling individual passengers would allow S6 to be checked directly, and symbolic or reduction techniques could push verification to larger request pools.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We designed a controller for Touchpoint Towers in mCRL2, consisting of a dispatcher, two elevators, an intersection manager and an emergency switch, and verified ten safety and four liveness requirements. All model-checked requirements hold for up to two outstanding requests, and the transfer order between wings holds by construction. Three lessons stand out. First, a formula can fail to terminate even on a finite state space if a quantifier or fixed-point parameter ranges over an infinite domain. Second, sanity checks are essential: without one, S9 would have been reported as verified on a bound where it is vacuous. Third, a state space that is too large to check directly can often still be verified per property, by keeping only the actions a formula mentions. The towers' fingers may touch; their elevators, at least, never do.
